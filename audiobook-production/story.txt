THE NOTE BELOW
An original work of fiction for listening

CHAPTER ONE. THE FALSE APPLAUSE.

At nine seventeen on the second Tuesday in November, a woman walked onto a stage, put down a brown paper bag, and received a standing ovation for doing absolutely nothing.

I had arranged it.

There are respectable ways to lose your job in the theater. You can drop a chandelier. You can put an actor through a trapdoor before the trapdoor is open. I knew a man who turned the microphones on during a marriage proposal that was supposed to be private. But I may be the only person in the history of the Victory Theater who was fired for applause.

The applause wasn't supposed to happen until later. That's the important part. I had a cassette of it. Eight seconds of people clapping and stamping their feet, borrowed from a boxing match, tucked into the sound desk for insurance. When I reached for the work light, my cuff caught the playback button. That was what I told everybody.

[AUDIO:FALSE_APPLAUSE]

It came out of the enormous old speakers like a train entering a room. Some people laughed. A few stood. Then more stood, because standing is something a person can do without knowing why. At the back, a man began to whistle. The woman onstage looked across two thousand heads at my booth. She couldn't see my face. I was behind glass. She raised one finger. Stop.

I stopped the tape. The real people were still applauding.

A man in a gray suit pushed past the front row, waving a sheet of paper. "Disqualified," he shouted. "She's disqualified before she starts."

He wasn't wrong.

But the woman in the winter coat took a tuning fork out of her bag, struck it against her knee, and waited for the applause to die. She had no pianist, no orchestra, and now no permission to perform. Her name was Edna Malloy. She was fifty-eight. Her left shoe had a square of black electrical tape over the toe. She'd been told, on national radio, that what she proposed to do could not be done.

At nine twenty-four she did it.

Or, to be exact, the audience did.

I have the original tape. The television station made a copy. So did the newspaper, though the reporter wrote that the audience had been hypnotized, which would have been convenient for the people who owned the speakers. Once in a while I play it for somebody. They invariably ask me to turn up the bass.

The bass isn't there.

For a while I hid the cassette from visitors. Later I began playing it for anyone who would listen. I still never told them the whole story.


CHAPTER TWO. THE CONTEST.

Six weeks earlier, in October, the Victory was for sale. You could smell that fact in the carpets.

For sixty-four years it had hosted touring orchestras, vaudeville, boxing, an escaped boa constrictor, and a political convention where two delegates tried to settle an argument with a bucket of wet paste. By nineteen ninety-eight the orchestra pit held folding chairs for a church group, the men running the bar watered the gin, and every Wednesday the owner asked me whether the ticket office computer could be made to print coupons for a nearby pizza place.

My name is Walter Lacey. Everybody called me Walt except my mother, who believed you should give a child a name too long to shout. I did sound. Not the artistic kind. The kind where someone calls you from the balcony because a man in the third row is hearing the singers through his fillings.

The contest was the idea of a loudspeaker company called Halden Audio. Halden had built a cabinet the size of a washing machine that, according to their advertisements, could produce a sound too low for any ordinary human to hear. You could feel it, though. A magician of a machine. They rented the Victory to demonstrate it, and to launch a new slogan: HEAR THE IMPOSSIBLE.

The slogan went up on banners where the plaster was coming down.

A local music teacher wrote to the company saying they hadn't invented anything. The human ear, she said, already heard impossible things all day. Halden's advertising director answered on morning radio that if anybody could produce their effect without a loudspeaker, he'd pay them fifty thousand dollars. He said it between a weather report and an offer for discounted truck tires. He expected the sentence to be forgotten by lunch.

The teacher turned up at our stage door at four forty with the radio recording on a cassette and a folding chair tied to the roof of her car.

"I'm Edna," she said. "Where's your electrical box?"

I showed her. She opened it and studied the wiring for three minutes, one hand behind her back. "I don't want your sockets," she said. "I want to make sure nobody else has them."

She taught music at a public school a little north of the city. She had no publicist. She kept pencils in an old cigar tube. She believed a note was a physical event and that most musicians got away with murder because people couldn't see it.

"What about the fifty thousand?" I asked.

"I'd like it," she said.

"For what?"

"Taxes."

She let that sit, watching me try to make it a better answer. Then she added, "And a new heating unit. If there's enough left over, I might buy some grapes."

It was hard to tell when Edna was being funny. You had to wait until her mouth moved.

She took two tuning forks from her bag. One she tapped lightly on the edge of my desk. A thin, plain sound came out, the kind you hear from a glass when somebody brushes a wet finger around its rim. The second fork was higher. She held them together and asked what I heard.

"Two forks."

"Well done. Now listen again."

She made a small adjustment with a rubber band and a wood clamp. Then she struck them once more.

For a moment I heard something underneath the forks. A low, rather ugly note. Not very loud. It seemed to be coming from the floor, which was impossible because the floor was concrete and I'd just checked for loose panels.

"What was that?" I asked.

"Good," she said. "You weren't expecting it."

She stopped the forks with her fingers. The low note stopped too.

I pulled the desk apart looking for a little concealed speaker. The next day I found out what she'd done. The sound below the two notes was a combination the hearing system could infer from their spacing. It was an old trick, old enough that people used it without knowing they were using it. A sound can have a pitch even when the vibration at that pitch isn't in the air. The ear supplies a missing bottom.

If you're trying to picture this, don't bother. It happens on every lousy little speaker that plays a voice sounding deeper than the speaker can physically reproduce. You've been living with it since childhood.

What unsettled me was the smile that came over Edna's face when she saw me crawling beneath the desk. She wasn't smiling at a trick. It was the face of someone watching a door open that had always been unlocked.

The Halden man, the one with the gray suit, arrived the next morning. His name was Peter Vail. He heard the demonstration and asked Edna to sign three forms. She crossed out half the words with a fountain pen that left blots on the page. There would be no concealed electronics, no recorded music, no planted performers, no subwoofers, and no devices beneath the stage.

"If there's one person helping you," Peter said, "the deal is off."

"One person?" Edna asked.

"Anybody at all."

She looked toward the empty seats. "I don't think you've thought that through."

He didn't like that. He'd spent eleven months working on a machine that could be moved with a forklift and was being challenged by someone who kept her instruments in a brown paper bag.

He set a date. November tenth. Seven-thirty doors, nine o'clock performance. The Victory sold every seat in four hours.

I told Edna I'd help with whatever she needed. I should explain why. Halden had offered to install a permanent new sound system in the theater if the event went well, and the owner had more or less promised me a steady salary to operate it. I'd worked freelance since I was twenty. I liked the idea of being paid in the months when no one wanted a magician.

I wanted Edna to win enough to make me look good, and lose enough for Halden to keep buying equipment.

I thought those were compatible wishes. I put two sets of cables in the same drawer and hoped they would never touch.


CHAPTER THREE. HOW HIGH CAN YOU GO.

Edna's first rehearsal involved three folding chairs and a paper cup of orange juice. I brought the chairs. She forgot the juice. A boy from the school named Ricky had been assigned to carry her music case, but she wouldn't let him near the stage.

"They said no helpers," she told him. "You can have a seat."

He was sixteen, had a tremendous mop of hair, and was missing an eyebrow from a homemade fireworks experiment. The whole town knew about it. When he wanted to avoid a difficult question he looked at your shoes.

I asked why Edna needed him if he wasn't allowed to perform.

"I wanted somebody here who might tell me I'm being an idiot," she said.

Ricky said, "You're being an idiot."

"Good. That's settled."

She sat us in the third row. From somewhere behind a curtain came a sound like a staircase being climbed. Not notes exactly. A rising series, each one a little higher than the one before it. It went on longer than a staircase has any right to. Ten seconds. Twenty. The sound grew thin and then fat again, and still it seemed to climb. At half a minute I leaned forward and thought that if she kept it up it would become a whistle that split the ceiling.

[AUDIO:RISING_TONE]

She stopped it.

"Where did it end?" she asked.

"Up there."

"Up where?"

"I don't know. High."

She opened the curtain. A little wooden box sat on a stool. Inside it was a toy mechanism connected to a set of chimes. It had gone round a circle. We had heard the pattern rise continually because the high chimes became soft while lower ones quietly took their places. The machine hadn't gone anywhere. After its last pass it was back at its starting point.

"Don't tell Halden I have this," she said. "It's rubbish."

Ricky was laughing so hard he fell against a chair.

I asked whether she'd use the box in the performance.

"No. The contract says no machines."

"Then why show us?"

She picked the box up with both hands. "Because I like to see what a room does when it thinks a thing is going somewhere."

There was some satisfaction in saying she was a music teacher. People imagine teachers as a supply of useful facts. Edna was more interested in how useful facts went wrong. A student could know every note on a page and still play the wrong ones if he was angry at his father. She could make that student practice for a month, and not once would she ask about the father. Then one afternoon she'd ask where the boy's hands wanted to go and stand there while he found out.

I didn't know this until Ricky told me. He said she was the first person who'd ever told him he played better when he wasn't trying to prove he belonged in the orchestra. "She said I had permission to be terrible for fifteen minutes," he said. "Then she told me to practice two hours."

That week Ricky stayed after school every evening to help her, without being asked.

For the concert, her plan was to use the hall itself. She visited every seat. She clapped into the empty orchestra pit and listened to the walls return the noise. She got on her hands and knees in a row of seats where the carpet smelled of old peanuts. She walked the balcony with a measuring tape, muttering about where the voices would sit.

She asked me to record a brief phrase, three notes, with no instrument beneath them. I did. When we played the tape back through the two tall Halden speakers, the lowest note appeared again. A dull, round pitch that seemed to press against my chest. But our display had nothing at that frequency. I made her repeat the recording three times. The meter stayed empty.

[AUDIO:PHANTOM]

"You're cheating," I said.

"Then find the wire."

I spent three evenings looking for it.

By the fourth I had stopped wanting the fifty thousand for her. I wanted her to tell me I wasn't stupid. Those two things aren't the same, though I treated them that way at the time.

On the final rehearsal night, Edna stood onstage and asked whether I believed the note existed.

"I heard it."

"That's not the question."

"If it isn't in the room, where is it?"

She scratched her wrist and looked up at the ceiling. There was a brown ring around the chandelier from the roof leak. The chandelier was an ugly thing somebody had donated because they wanted their name on a brass plaque.

"I don't know exactly where a thought is," she said. "I don't worry about that every time I have one."

I asked whether this was a physics experiment or a concert.

"It had better be a concert," she said. "People have paid for parking."


CHAPTER FOUR. THE MAN IN THE BOOTH.

The mistake happened because the Victory had two ways to start a sound. One button was square and red. The other was square and red and had been installed upside down.

I'd been meaning to label them since February.

By six on the night of the show, I'd found three things to worry about. The local television news was here, with cables stretched through the aisle. Peter Vail had brought two inspectors to check for hidden electronics and had given each one a little flashlight with the company logo. And there was a hiss in the main speaker, a thin sound that I knew could mean an amplifier was starting to die. The Victory's owner told me to find a way of making it sound like wind.

At six forty-five Edna came in, still wearing the winter coat. She didn't take off her gloves. She had no music bag this time, just the paper sack and two wooden blocks.

Peter met her by the side curtain.

"No accomplices," he reminded her.

"I know."

"And remember the agreement. If your audience sings, that's a choir. A choir is an instrument."

"Then they ought to get paid," she said.

"You don't get to redefine the terms after we've agreed on them."

"I didn't realize I'd agreed to own their mouths."

He walked away as though that had settled things in his favor.

She found me in the booth. I had my tape deck open and was running a finger over the capstan. I should have closed it when she came in. Instead I told her what it was.

"Eight seconds of applause," I said. "The television people want some crowd noise for the opening."

"Why? There'll be a crowd."

"You'd be surprised how shy a crowd sounds through a microphone."

"No," she said, "I don't think I would."

She put her hands on the glass. The house was filling up below us. Coats came off, seats folded, somebody spilled a drink and tried to blame the slope of the floor.

"Walt," she said. "You'll promise me one thing."

I was already braced for a request about audio cables.

"Don't play any of it. If the room likes what I do, we'll know. And if it doesn't, I want to know that too."

I agreed. Absolutely. I could see why she cared. She was about to risk being laughed at by a full theater and a television camera. It would be indecent to give her a false answer.

At seven thirty Peter started pressing for a pre-recorded introduction. I told him Edna wanted none. He said we couldn't have two thousand people sit down with no music and no announcement. I asked why. He said that's not how these things are done.

We compromised on three bells.

We were running sixteen minutes late. Peter had made his inspectors test the outlets three times, and an old circuit breaker had refused to reset. At nine sixteen I set the tape behind the desk, beyond easy reach. Edna waited in the wing with the bag. Ricky was in the cheap seats, wearing a tie his mother had tied so badly that the knot sat near one ear. In the front row Peter sat between the two inspectors, their flashlights on their laps like little pistols.

The house lights dipped. Three bells. I turned on the work light for Edna.

And caught the wrong button with my cuff. That was my account of it, and I stuck to it for years.

The sound slammed through the room. Two thousand people reacted to an audience that wasn't there. They laughed, then applauded, then began to stand. You could watch it move. A man in the seventh row checked the balcony before rising. The woman next to him saw him rise and came up more quickly. In one of the boxes a girl stood on a seat to see what everyone was standing for.

It was twelve seconds before I found the stop button. Edna had been looking at me the whole time.

Peter was out of his seat. By the time he reached the stage he had the contract in his hand.

"That's a recording," he announced. "Your entire demonstration has been contaminated."

The room quieted in pieces. Nobody likes discovering that they've been given instructions they didn't notice receiving.

"She's out," Peter told the television man. "You heard it. We all heard it."

I could have stayed in the booth. Every person in the building would have assumed Edna had asked for the sound. She had no contract with Halden that the audience would read. The owner might have backed me. I might even have kept the job.

I opened the booth door.

It was an ordinary door, green paint peeling from the bottom. I'd opened it perhaps ten thousand times. I left it swinging behind me. I had never done that. The automatic closer had been broken for years.

On the stage I took the microphone and said, "I played the tape."

Somebody in the back yelled, "We can tell, mate." It was Buffalo, but the man somehow sounded British. There are people who save up their funniest voice for occasions when nobody asked for it.

"She told me not to. She didn't know I had it. I'm sorry."

Peter put a hand on the microphone stand. "I'm not trying to make a moral judgment. The rules are the rules."

"You can have the rules," Edna said. "Give me the room."

The owner had come down the side aisle. I saw him calculate a refund for two thousand seats. I saw him calculate the local television station's bill. He nodded at me.

"Five minutes," he said. "Then we're stopping the show."

I went back to the booth and turned off every speaker in the building. I pulled the mains. The sound desk's tiny red lamps vanished. For the first time in my working life I was in a theater I could not control.

In the dark I caught my hand reaching for a fader that was no longer lit.


CHAPTER FIVE. THE NOTE BELOW.

Edna waited until the last squeak of a folding seat had stopped. Without the amplification you could hear one of the televisions in the lobby leaking the local news through a closed door. You could hear a little electrical ticking from a defective EXIT sign. You could hear somebody unwrap a candy with the care of a burglar.

She took two wooden blocks from her bag. The wood was pale, like a cutting board. She struck them together twice, and a small clear crack came back off the ceiling.

"I was meant to show you a note," she said. "I'm going to need you to help me find it."

Peter stood up. She looked at him.

"If that makes it against the rules, you may leave. It's your money."

The applause that followed was real but not very good. A smattering of people clapped, mostly out of sympathy. It died quickly. Edna smiled at the sound like an actress welcoming an honest review.

She asked a little cluster of people at the front to hum a pitch. It came out ugly, as such things do when strangers have to find the same note. She asked the middle rows for a different pitch, a bit higher. They were shy and made a mess. Somebody sang too loudly and then pretended to cough. Ricky, up in the back, gave a low, magnificent note and was told by his friends to shut up.

Edna didn't get angry. She made them start again. As they repeated the two tones, the gap between them became steady. The hum from the balcony joined the hum below. I had never heard the Victory carry sound this way. The plaster and velvet swallowed part of each tone and returned another part in a new direction. There were voices coming back from corners where none of my microphones had ever picked up a thing.

[AUDIO:PHANTOM]

Then I heard it.

Not loud. I leaned against the back wall to be sure. Beneath what the room was actually singing was a deeper note, one that no single section had been asked to produce. A low C that seemed to gather in the soles of my shoes. I had no power, no microphone, and no loudspeaker to blame. I knew the thing the brain was doing. I'd read Edna's books. Knowing didn't diminish it at all.

She made a hand signal and the balcony rose in pitch. The bass beneath them moved too, following the intervals. When she swept her hands upward, the audience's voices went higher, then folded back into one another. The sensation was of ascending without reaching a top. I'd heard the mechanical version in rehearsal. This was something else. You could hear individual people working at it. A voice went wrong and recovered. Someone laughed midway through a note. The crowd corrected itself, not all at once, and with nobody deciding for the others.

[AUDIO:RISING_TONE]

Edna turned around on the platform and faced the curtains. For nearly half a minute she conducted with her back to the room, and then lowered her hands. The hum continued. The audience hadn't noticed the signal.

She waited.

This was the moment I almost ruined everything again. My fingers knew where the mixer faders should have been. I had spent an adult lifetime knowing whether a room was working by seeing little red lights. Without them I was lost. I wanted a button so badly I could feel it in my palm.

An inspector rose from his seat. He was trying to determine whether a recording was playing. He held the little Halden flashlight up as though it might illuminate a sound. He found nothing but two thousand faces, some closed-eyed and some making the strangest expressions of effort. A little boy in the aisle was standing rigid, mouth wide, humming a pitch almost an octave off. His father was trying to stop him. Edna saw this and shook her head at the father. The boy kept going.

A moment later there was a new sound in the room.

[AUDIO:REAL_NOTE]

It was not mystical. It was not even particularly beautiful. The crowd had stumbled on the bottom note itself, some people making it while the others supplied the upper notes. Now there was an actual vibration at the pitch we'd been imagining. The room was making the note for real.

Edna dropped her arms. Two people kept humming. Then five. Then half the hall. You could hear a voice crack, a shoe scrape, one person take a breath, and the whole thing lean toward an ending that no one had written down.

The note stopped.

An inspector in the front row scratched his pen across the contract. We heard each stroke.

Then Ricky stood up.

He was not first. That's another thing the newspaper got wrong. A woman three seats ahead had been standing for perhaps ten seconds already. She was holding the hand of a man who couldn't get himself up from his seat. They were trying to rise together. The woman kept her arm under his elbow until he got his feet beneath him. The man got his weight forward, and with a small grunt he stood.

Ricky saw them. His friends saw Ricky. The row beside them came up, then the balcony, and within moments the floor of the Victory was making the tremendous loose thunder of two thousand feet. I watched the tide pass across the seats. It looked very much like the wave my tape had caused, earlier. Only now no one was being told where to look.

[AUDIO:REAL_APPLAUSE]

Peter Vail did not stand. He was writing something on the contract. He would later tell the reporter that audience participation constituted the use of additional instruments, making the demonstration ineligible for the prize. I can't prove he was wrong. The contract was badly written, but not as badly as I wanted it to be.

Edna didn't collect fifty thousand dollars. She went home in a car with a broken heater and a bag that had begun to tear at the bottom.

I have been asked what she won. She didn't win the wager. It was a genuine defeat and she was angry about it. For months she wrote letters to Peter asking him to pay for the part of the evening before I spoiled it. He replied to every letter on company stationery. None of his replies contained a check.


CHAPTER SIX. THE OTHER BUTTON.

Peter's company kept the money. The paper ran a photograph of Edna beside a speaker she had never touched. Underneath it was the headline LOCAL TEACHER LOSES AUDIO CHALLENGE. There was an argument about whether the audience had cheated. A professor from the university wrote a splendidly furious letter about the science. Nobody printed it. The owner of the Victory thanked me for my years of service and told me not to come back on Monday.

For the next fourteen months I fixed supermarket public-address systems. The pay was adequate. It was difficult to take pride in the way a supermarket tells people about discounted chicken.

Peter Vail stayed in audio. His company put out a line of small black speakers for home theaters, and people bought them. When I saw a pair in a shop window I went inside and listened. They were good. I would like to tell you they weren't. The bass was especially fine.

Ricky went to university on a music scholarship. His eyebrow grew back. He sent me a photograph from a station in Poland, standing beside five people whose names he'd written so badly I couldn't read them. He was holding a violin. He had been a clarinet player at the Victory, but in Poland he'd decided to learn something else. In the photograph, he looked like a man who had missed the last train and didn't mind.

Edna kept teaching. She wrote once to say she'd finally got the heating unit but no grapes. I visited her almost ten years after the show. Her kitchen was a disaster. One window was stuck open, and the water had to run for a minute before it got warm. She sat at the table cutting up a pear with a butter knife.

She asked if I still had the tapes.

"I've got all of them."

"The good one?"

"Yes."

"The stupid one?"

I nodded. I had tried to throw away the first cassette twice. I'd taken it out of its box and stood over the bin with it in my hand. Each time I remembered her looking up at my booth and put it back.

She cut the pear in half. The knife struck the plate.

"Edna," I said. "The tape didn't go on by accident."

She didn't stop cutting.

"I pushed the button. I knew which one was which. There was nothing wrong with the desk."

She held the knife in the air for a moment. Then she put it down.

"Why?"

"Halden had promised to keep me on if the theater got the contract. They had all that equipment. I didn't want you to make it look useless."

She looked at me for an uncomfortably long time. I had rehearsed explanations for this, a great many of them. All seemed suddenly as absurd as rehearsing a sneeze.

"I spent twelve seconds deciding whether to stop it," I said.

"I know," she said.

"You knew?"

"I've been to your booth, Walt. You can turn something off faster than that."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

"There was a concert to get on with."

She returned to the pear. She didn't forgive me. She didn't say that what I'd done had turned out for the best. If I'd come for either of those things, I'd have left disappointed.

"Did you know they could do it?" I asked after a while. "The low note, I mean."

"I knew two forks could do it. I knew the room would help. I didn't know they'd start singing the bottom note themselves. Did you?"

"No."

"Well. Good for them."

She passed me a piece of pear. There was a brown mark on it that she'd cut around, though not very carefully. I ate it. We talked about the heating unit. The rest of the afternoon was unremarkable.

There is no reason for you to be interested in what happened to me after that. I was the sound man, not the act. But it explains something about the last bit of tape, so I'll tell you.

Ricky came home the next spring. He was organizing a little concert for the school, with children who couldn't yet keep a beat and two people from the community band who could. Edna asked me to arrange the chairs. I arrived early. She put a borrowed clarinet into my hands and said the boy who'd planned to play it was sick.

"I can't play this," I told her.

"Well, that's going to be interesting," she said.

She showed me three notes. My first attempt sounded like an injured goose. She made me repeat them. The sound improved by a miserable fraction. Every time I stopped to apologize she told me to keep going, not kindly but firmly, like a person trying to push a car out of snow.

I asked why she was bothering with me.

"Ricky wanted four voices," she said. "We've only got three."

I ended up in the back row between a trumpet player with a pink face and a little girl whose feet didn't touch the floor. The first piece was terrible. I missed my opening by half a measure and lost the reed twice. The girl laughed at me. The trumpet player gave me a look that would have frightened a better musician.

On the second piece, Edna stood in the center with her arms at her sides. She waited for us to begin. Nobody played. The trumpet player was adjusting his mouthpiece. Somebody in the audience coughed. I kept looking toward the sound desk, an empty table with no equipment on it.

The girl beside me whispered, "You're supposed to start."

I took a breath.

[AUDIO:PHANTOM]

What came out was just the first note. Too thin. A little sharp. But it belonged to me. Someone answered it. The other two instruments came in. Soon the trumpet came in from the other side and the whole thing began to move. I could hear the girl beside me breathe in before her note. My next breath followed hers. I was too busy to be frightened.

I don't remember whether the audience clapped. There were forty-three people. Edna counted them afterward because the school needed the money from the door. Edna was counting them afterward because the school needed the money from the door. I remember the girl saying, "See?" and putting the clarinet away for me before I could break it.

Here is the thing about the two recordings from the Victory. I used to say I couldn't always tell which ovation was fake. I was lying about that too, though in a smaller way.

On the first tape, just before the shouting begins, there is a hard click. You have to listen closely. It's the little plastic playback button under my finger.

[AUDIO:BUTTON_CLICK]

On the other tape you can hear the audience breathing. There is an old theater seat folding up. There's a boy whistling completely out of tune, and someone three rows away laughing at him. Then a single clap. Another. A woman tells the person beside her to get up, and they do.

[AUDIO:REAL_APPLAUSE]

I know which one I started.

I still listen to the other one because I don't know where it begins.
