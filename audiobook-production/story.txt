THE PART I SOLD
Original speculative fiction

CHAPTER ONE. SOMEONE ELSE'S LIFE.

The woman who came to tell me I was a murderer had a parking ticket in her fist.

She put it on my counter next to a jar of coffee grounds and said, "Is there anywhere around here to park that isn't illegal?"

"No," I said.

"Good. That gives us something in common."

She sat without being invited. Forty or thereabouts. Navy coat. One of those metal lunch tins that used to come with a cartoon on the side. Hers was scratched to silver. She placed it between her feet and kept the handle hooked under one heel.

I was working on a customer's first kiss. He'd paid to spend another six minutes being seventeen. The memory had arrived with a smell of bleach that wasn't supposed to be there. Whoever had sold it had been standing too close to a swimming-pool drain. The customer wanted the bleach removed.

There was nothing I could do about it. You get the whole six minutes. The nervous system doesn't separate the girl's mouth from the chemicals in the air any more than it separates your mother's voice from the room in which she spoke. You can soften the feeling at the edges. You can't clean the past.

I told the woman I was busy.

"I know," she said. "You've been busy for seventeen years."

She opened her lunch tin. Inside was a piece of paper folded into a square, a packet of salt crackers, and a small black memory cartridge. An old one, with a brass lip that had been phased out before most people learned to stop calling them tapes.

She put the cartridge on the counter.

"I bought this in Cleveland. I was twenty-three. They told me it was the memory of a man who'd saved somebody's life. They said it would give me courage. I had never stood up to anybody, and I was tired of myself."

I glanced at the serial marks. Somebody had scraped the name off.

"Did it work?" I asked.

"I spent six years trying to get it out of my head."

She looked at the ceiling until her eyes stopped shining.

"There's a loading dock. It's raining. There's a man in the water below it, screaming. I have his hand. He begs me. I let go."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. You haven't heard the worst part."

She reached into her coat, unfolded a newspaper clipping, and pushed it toward me. The photograph showed a man of about thirty with a large nose and a white mark above his upper lip. He was standing beside a ferry, smiling straight at the camera. The headline said his body had been found downstream two days after he disappeared.

The woman tapped the photograph.

"That's the man in my memory."

I waited. Outside a truck hit a pothole hard enough to rattle the windows.

"Then the memory's real," I said.

"Yes."

"Take it to the police."

"I did. Three times. The last detective asked whether I'd tried sleeping more."

She watched me look at the name again. Sam Kessler. I knew it. It belonged to a man who had once lent me a wrench and never asked for it back.

I had worked with Sam when I was twenty. We loaded freight at the river terminal. He showed me how to make a diesel engine cough itself back to life. He ate boiled eggs by cutting them in half with a pocketknife. He was the only person there who called everyone by their full first name, even when they hated it.

"Sam died in an accident," I said.

"That's what the paper says."

She pushed a second photograph across the counter. It was a grainy picture of a young man on the dock, taken at a company picnic. Hair falling over one eye. Green jacket. Left hand shoved deep into a pocket, the way boys stand when they don't know how to stand.

Me.

"I found you last week," she said. "I kept looking until I found you."

"That doesn't prove anything."

She leaned closer. "Your left ring finger doesn't straighten," she said. "There's a spot under the knuckle that burns when you grip something hard. I felt it when I was holding Sam. I still get the pain if I close my fist the wrong way."

I turned my hand over. The finger had been crooked since a fall at work. I kept forgetting how visible it was. Dina was looking at it with the expression of a person examining an old injury of her own.

The woman looked away from my face. She was giving me a moment to breathe.

"My name is Dina," she said. "The memory is yours. And I'd like you to take it back."

CHAPTER TWO. THE EXCHANGE.

People who have never traded memories imagine a bank. They picture shelves with jars on them, each jar containing a blue light, or perhaps a room full of white machines humming gently while a scientist explains consciousness to a wealthy patron.

My shop used to sell swimming-pool equipment. The counter was the old cash register stand. I kept the transfer chairs in the back, next to a mop sink that leaked all winter. If a customer asked for privacy, I closed a curtain that had belonged to a carpet showroom. The curtain smelled of dust and wet feet.

A memory transfer costs the donor the ability to recall that stretch of time. The recipient gets it as vividly as if it happened to them. You cannot manufacture a second copy. There is one living owner at any point, although people can remember talking about an event after they've given away the event itself. That's why someone who has sold a wedding still knows they're married. They just don't remember getting married.

When the practice began, people sold things they didn't want. Painful accidents. Deathbeds. Divorce arguments. After a few years, a market appeared for the things people did want. First kisses. Concerts. Grand slams. A retired golfer sold the afternoon he had won his championship for a sum that would have bought his entire golf club. He still displayed the trophy. He simply had no idea what it felt like to lift it.

Some customers found that funny. Others came back crying.

I got into the business because I was good at handling the cartridges. They contain a fragile pattern, and there's a way of fitting the little brass lip into the machine without bending it. I learned to do it in the dark. I also got into the business because there was money in it, and I liked money better than I liked most of the things it bought.

Dina listened while I explained how the old serial marks worked. They recorded the donor's nervous signature, a pattern as personal as a fingerprint. Scraping the label didn't remove the pattern.

"So you can prove it?" she asked.

"I can read the original mark."

"Then show me."

I went into the back and returned with a narrow metal reader. The cartridge slid home with a click. The screen displayed three lines. Transfer date. Original length. Donor mark.

I had seen that mark before. It was the one on my old license.

I sat down.

"How much did I get for it?" I asked.

"You don't know?"

"How much?"

"The receipt says forty thousand."

I stared at the number. At twenty I had been sleeping on a mattress in the room above a tire shop. Forty thousand dollars was not the kind of money I could have forgotten spending. Yet I had a blank stretch of time in that year, and a memory of a bank teller counting out a large amount of cash to me. I'd always assumed I'd won an insurance settlement. I had lost the paperwork when I moved.

"I sold it," I said.

Dina nodded. There was no triumph in her face. She'd known that before she came in.

"Why?" she asked.

I tried to remember the week Sam disappeared. The loading dock in the rain. A yellow rope hanging from a cleat. The dull knock of the ferry against its bumper. I could remember the day before, when Sam borrowed a sandwich and promised to pay me back. I could remember the day after, when the police came and asked questions. Between those two days there was a hole. I could remember being questioned by the police. I could not remember what I had said to them.

"I don't know," I said.

"You sold it two days after he died."

I pushed the cartridge back toward her. "Take it to somebody else."

"I have."

"Then keep trying."

Dina took the packet of crackers from her tin, broke one in half, and ate it without enthusiasm.

"You ever see a man drown?" she asked.

"No."

"I have. Every night for seventeen years. I have a husband who thinks I was in a bad accident. I have a dog that sleeps under my bed because I wake up shouting. I know exactly how a drowning man feels when he's got hold of somebody and the somebody lets go. I can feel the skin slipping under my fingers."

She stopped. The shop refrigerator rattled in the corner.

"I didn't do that," she said. "You did."

I wanted to argue. I could not find a decent place to start.

She stood up and picked up her tin.

"I'll give you until tomorrow," she said. "Then I'm taking what I have to the man who found Sam's body. He was a harbor officer. He's retired, but he's still alive. He believes there was another person on that dock."

"If you're right, that's what you should do."

"I am right."

She left the cartridge on the counter.

The parking officer had put another ticket under her windshield wiper. She pulled it off, stared at it, and stuck it in her coat pocket. Then she drove away.

I sat in my old pool-supply shop for a long time. Before closing, I wrote Sam's name on the back of an invoice. Beneath it I wrote the date of the transfer. I kept trying to picture the river, but every time I reached the edge of it my mind went in another direction. I found myself calculating what forty thousand dollars in old money would be worth now. I hated that I was doing it.

CHAPTER THREE. THE DOCK.

The river terminal had changed. The freight sheds were gone, replaced by a row of glass buildings that looked like fish tanks. The old dock remained because the city had run out of money before it could be demolished. It was fenced off, its boards warped and gray. Grass grew through the cracks. The big yellow cleat I'd remembered was still there, crusted with rust, like a tooth left in a jaw after everything else had been removed.

I drove down after sunset, parked beside a dumpster, and climbed through a gap in the fence. The river smelled of mud and gasoline. A bus passed on the road above the terminal, throwing a moment of light across the water. Farther out, a ferry crossed toward the opposite bank. Its windows were little rectangles of warmth, moving through the dark.

I had no plan. I was hoping for a small miracle, perhaps a forgotten security camera or an old sign that would prove I hadn't been there. What I found was a narrow metal ladder bolted to the side of the dock. It descended toward the water and disappeared into it.

I remembered that ladder. I remembered painting it red. I'd done it on a summer afternoon while Sam stood above me shouting about the drips.

There was a padlock on a maintenance shed. I broke it with a piece of loose pipe. Inside were empty paint tins, torn tarpaulins, and a cabinet full of damp paper. The drawers had swollen shut. I pried one open and began pulling out folders, searching for a log book, a payroll sheet, anything that might contain my name.

A voice behind me said, "You won't find it there."

I turned. An old man stood in the doorway with a flashlight in one hand and a heavy ring of keys in the other. He wore a dark blue cap pulled down over his ears. His right leg was stiff, and he leaned on the frame as if it were a rail.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"The man the city still pays to look after this mess. I heard you break the lock."

He shone the light at my face. "Ray. Good God. I thought you were dead."

His name was Len Carter. He'd worked nights for the harbor office. I remembered him as a man who always had a cigarette attached to his lower lip and could tell the weather by smelling the river. Now the cigarette was gone, replaced by a hard little cough.

"What are you doing here?" he asked.

"Looking for something."

"You'd have been better looking at home. They spray for rats down here."

He led me to the edge of the dock. We stood where the yellow cleat faced the water. I told him a woman had come to see me. I told him she had bought a memory of Sam dying.

Len stared at the river for so long that I wondered if he'd heard me.

"I knew somebody was with him," he said.

"How?"

"There were two sets of wet footprints on the planks. The police said one pair might be from the rescue crew, but I got there before the rescue crew. I saw them myself."

"Did you tell them?"

"Of course."

"What did they do?"

"They wrote it down. That's what people do when they can't think of anything else."

I asked whether he still had the report. He shook his head. The old files had been sent to a county warehouse and then lost during a move.

"But I have something else," he said. "At home. It isn't far."

Len lived in an apartment above the old harbor office. The staircase smelled of boiled cabbage, and he stopped halfway up to give his knee a rest. At the top he unlocked the door and went straight to a kitchen drawer crammed with spare batteries and rubber bands. From a biscuit tin he took a folded sheet of carbon paper. The letters were faint, almost rubbed away. A rescue call, made at eleven twenty-three on the night Sam died.

"I kept a copy," he said. "I thought there would be questions later. Then nobody came to ask them."

I checked it twice. The caller was listed as an unidentified male. He had reported a person in the water near the freight terminal. He said he was going down after him.

"That was you," Len said.

I looked up. "How do you know?"

"I heard your voice. I was on the radio that night. You were shouting so hard I could hardly understand you."

"What happened?"

"You jumped."

I felt something move inside my chest, a small change in pressure.

"I jumped?"

"Off the dock. Into the river. I saw you come out of the water with the rescue boys. You couldn't stand up. They wrapped you in a blanket. You kept saying you had him, that you'd had his hand."

I pictured the ladder below the dock. The memory Dina carried ended with a hand slipping away. What Len had given me was the next part. The part I had never known how to ask about. The rescue took place beyond the cut, during minutes I'd lost to shock and cold. I had told myself there was nothing worth recovering there.

"Did I push him?" I asked.

Len looked offended. "How would I know? I wasn't there when he went in."

"But you saw me jump."

"Yes."

I looked out over the river. There was no way to make the two pieces fit neatly together. I might have pushed Sam and jumped after him. I might have tried to save him and failed. I might have done both, one after the other. The river had taken Sam, and a machine had taken the part of me that knew what happened before.

"Why didn't you tell me?" I asked.

"I did," Len said. "You kept asking me the same question the next day. I thought you were in shock. Then you stopped coming around."

The carbon paper lay between us on Len's kitchen table. A ferry horn sounded downstream. It was a low, unhappy noise.

"What do you remember about Sam?" Len asked.

I looked at him.

"He lent me a wrench once. Red handle. The jaw had a chip in it. I can still see him handing it to me down on the dock."

"Maybe," Len said. "Or you saw him hand it to me. Sam lent the same tools to everybody. Try to remember something he actually said."

"He hated being cold," I said. "He wore a coat indoors. He used to slice his eggs with a pocketknife."

Len smiled. "He could never sharpen that thing. Every winter I told him to buy a better knife."

For a moment we both laughed. Then the laughter stopped. Len folded the carbon paper and slid it across the table for me to keep.

CHAPTER FOUR. A TRADE.

I went back to the shop and asked the reader for a preview.

A preview doesn't give you the memory. It lends you the sensation for a few seconds and then cuts the connection. You can tell somebody what you saw, the way you can tell them a dream, but your body doesn't keep it. That is why people pay for the real thing.

I sat in the transfer chair, fitted the cartridge, and turned the small brass dial. The reader hummed. I closed my eyes.

Rain on my face. A hand in mine. Sam Kessler below the dock, feet kicking in black water. He was shouting something I couldn't hear over the river. My sleeve was soaked. My fingers hurt from gripping his wrist. I pulled. He rose an inch, then fell back. I tried again. The skin of his arm was slick.

The anger came before I had time to ask whose it was. Later I would have called it mine. At that moment it belonged to no one I could name. It's a stupid thing to admit, but it's true. I was angry that he weighed so much, angry that he had gone in, angry that he was making me stand there in the rain with my back screaming from the effort. Then he looked up at me with an expression so frightened that I wanted to be anywhere else.

My grip shifted.

His hand slid.

I let go.

There are ten seconds in that memory that I have never been able to describe. The river was dark. Sam's face moved away. I stood above him with my hand open, trying to decide whether I'd opened it on purpose.

Then the memory ended.

I came out of the chair sweating. My fingers were bent into a claw. I could feel river water in my sleeves, though the fabric was dry. I tore the headset off and sat bent forward until the room stopped turning.

Dina arrived the next morning. She was wearing the same navy coat. She'd brought a fresh parking ticket and a paper bag of doughnuts. She put them on the counter without comment.

"Did you listen?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Then you know."

"I know what those eleven minutes feel like."

"That's not an answer."

"Len Carter saw me jump into the river after Sam. I tried to save him."

Dina stared at me. "You jumped?"

"That's what he says. He had the radio call."

She sat down slowly, as if the chair had moved beneath her.

"I never knew that," she said.

"Neither did I."

For a while we ate doughnuts. They were stale and had too much sugar on them. Dina ate hers in small bites, looking at the floor. I could see her trying to rearrange seventeen years of terror around a new fact. I knew better than to tell her she should feel relieved.

"Can you give it back?" she asked.

"Yes."

"I want you to."

"There has to be something in exchange."

She nodded. She knew the rules. A memory leaves one person and enters another. To take those eleven minutes back, I had to give her eleven minutes of my life. They could be ordinary minutes. They could be wonderful or miserable. The machine didn't care. It only needed a piece of living time to hold the space open.

I offered her a childhood summer, the afternoon I learned to swim in a quarry. She shook her head. I offered the day I won a flying lesson in a raffle. She asked whether I was making a joke. I offered money. She pushed it back.

"I don't want a good memory," she said. "I want yours."

"They are all mine."

"I want last night. The eleven minutes with Len, when he told you what happened after you let go. I want to know what it was like for you to hear that you'd jumped."

I didn't answer at once. The request was cruel. I could see that she knew it. She had spent seventeen years carrying my fear, and now she wanted to keep the moment she'd learned where it came from.

"If I give you that," I said, "I won't remember talking to Len."

"You can write it down."

"Writing isn't remembering."

"No," she said. "It isn't."

I looked at the cartridge on the counter. The little brass lip had a dent in it. I had fitted hundreds of cartridges, and I knew exactly how to straighten that dent. It would take about eight seconds.

Dina waited.

"All right," I said.

She didn't smile. She just took off her coat and folded it over the back of the chair.

CHAPTER FIVE. THE ELEVEN MINUTES.

We made the trade at noon. The shop refrigerator was running. A delivery truck had parked outside with its engine idling. The sunlight came through the front window and made a yellow rectangle on the floor.

Dina sat in the left chair. I sat in the right. I fitted the headsets and checked the connections. The machine was old, but it was mine, and I trusted it more than I trusted people who claimed to understand how it worked. I placed the cartridge in the cradle between us.

"You'll get the river," I told her. "I'll get last night."

"No," she said. "The other way around."

I looked at the controls. She was right. I had switched the leads. My hands were shaking. I hadn't noticed.

"Good catch," I said.

She nodded once. "I've had seventeen years to practice remembering details."

I swapped the leads.

"Before we do this," I said, "I want you to know that I'm going back to the harbor office. I'm going to tell them what I remember."

"Even if you don't know whether you pushed him?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

I thought about that. I could have told her I wanted justice for Sam. I could have said I wanted to be a better man. Those would have sounded reasonable. I also wanted to find out what kind of man I had been. I didn't like not knowing. That was less noble, but it was true.

"Because he died," I said. "And I was there."

Dina looked at me for a long time. Then she put on the headset.

The machine started. There was a soft pressure behind my eyes, like a sneeze that never came. My fingers went numb. The light from the window seemed to move sideways. For an instant I smelled river mud and cold rain. Then it was gone.

I opened my eyes.

Dina was sitting opposite me with tears on her face. She had both hands over her mouth. The cartridge was empty. The little screen showed that the exchange had completed.

I knew who she was. I knew she'd come about a memory. I knew that Sam Kessler had died years ago and that I'd sold the moment of his death. I remembered going to the dock the night before. I remembered driving home. Between those two memories was a gap where I'd spoken to Len Carter and learned that I'd jumped into the river.

Dina looked at me as if she had just met somebody she'd been married to for a long time.

"You were afraid," she said.

"Of course."

"No. I mean you were afraid that you'd let go on purpose. You were afraid the memory would tell you that you were a murderer."

I waited. She wiped her face with the sleeve of her coat.

"And then Len told you that you jumped," she said. "You were so relieved you could hardly stand. You wanted to laugh, but you were ashamed of being relieved. You kept asking him whether he was sure."

I tried to remember that feeling. It was like reaching for a word in another language. I knew there was a word. I couldn't make it appear.

Dina leaned forward. "Ray. I know now."

She said it softly. She had those minutes in Len's kitchen in her head, and I had her seventeen-year nightmare. I looked at the empty cartridge and tried to remember why I'd moved the chairs so close together. I could remember the reason as a sentence. I couldn't remember believing it.

My first thought was that she had cheated me. I knew that made no sense. I'd been the one who set the machine. Still, I wanted the missing part of last night back, and the person who could give it to me was sitting three feet away. I had her old memory of the river now, vivid enough to smell the rain. I could feel Sam's wrist turning in my hand. I understood, with a sharp little jolt of fear, that I no longer remembered agreeing to own this feeling.

"Will you still go to the harbor office?" she asked.

I looked at the cartridge. The brass lip had a small dent. I could fix it in eight seconds.

"I said I would," I told her.

"You don't remember saying it."

"No."

"Then why?"

I looked at the sunlight on the floor. It had moved while we were in the chairs. The delivery truck outside pulled away. Somewhere in the building a pipe banged, three slow knocks.

"Because you're here," I said. "And you're telling me what happened."

She nodded. That was enough for her. It was going to have to be enough for me.

CHAPTER SIX. WHAT REMAINS.

I'm Dina. The woman in the navy coat. The one with the parking tickets.

The first time I heard this story I thought Ray was making it up. His voice sounded strange to me, as if I were listening to somebody reading a letter over the telephone. He had asked me to make a recording of what happened. He said he'd need a witness who wasn't inside his own head.

I did not want to be his witness. I wanted to go home. I wanted to sleep through the night without seeing Sam Kessler's face in the water. I had spent years hating a man I'd never met, and now I had met him, and he wasn't the monster I had made of him. He was worse in some ways. He was ordinary. He was nervous about money. He complained about the cost of coffee. He had a loose screw in the drawer of his desk that he kept meaning to fix.

After the trade, he went to the harbor office. I went with him because I didn't trust him to go alone. The young clerk behind the desk had never heard of Sam. She made us fill out a form. Then she called somebody older, and we waited on plastic chairs beneath a television showing a cooking program with the sound off.

Ray told the officer what he knew. He described the dock, the rain, Sam's hand. He told them about the memory sale and the exchange. He said he didn't know whether he'd pushed Sam into the river. He said he wanted the old case opened.

The officer asked if he had proof.

Ray put the cartridge on the desk. Then he took out the carbon copy of the rescue call Len had given him. He laid it beside the cartridge. The two things looked ridiculous together, a piece of old paper and a little black object hardly bigger than a matchbox.

"There," he said. "That's what I've got."

The officer promised to make inquiries. It was not a satisfying promise. He did not take out handcuffs. He did not say Ray was innocent. He did not say that Sam had died because of him. He put the papers in a folder and asked for a phone number.

Outside, Ray stood in the sun, blinking. There were people walking past with shopping bags, a man arguing with a parking meter, a woman trying to keep a toddler from running into the road. Nobody knew what had happened inside. The world had the nerve to continue being the world.

"Do you feel better?" I asked him.

"I don't know."

I thought about the memory he had given me. Eleven minutes of his life. I could feel his relief when Len told him he'd jumped into the river. I could feel the shame that followed, the fear that relief itself was a kind of betrayal. Those feelings were mine now. I had not earned them. I had bought them with a nightmare.

"Would you take it back?" I asked.

He shook his head. "I couldn't. You'd have to give up the river again."

"I know."

We walked toward the parking lot. My car had another ticket under the wiper. I pulled it free and laughed. Ray looked at me as if laughter were a language he'd forgotten how to speak.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing," I said. "It's just expensive being alive."

He smiled a little. We stood there for a while, two people who had shared a death and a night, neither of us certain where one life ended and the other began.

When I got home, my husband asked whether I'd sorted out the thing that had been troubling me. He'd learned not to ask too many questions. I told him I had. Then I went upstairs, lay down on the bed, and waited for the river to come.

It didn't.

I remembered Ray at Len's kitchen table, holding a carbon copy of the rescue call. I remembered his face as he asked whether Len was sure. The memory ended before Len had finished answering. That was all I'd bought. Eleven minutes, cut from somebody else's night.

Sometime after midnight I got out of bed to check the kitchen lock. My husband was asleep. There was a wet dishcloth by the sink, and the window was cracked open just enough for the wind to move the curtain. I shut it. I remember doing that. I was there.

In the morning my husband asked whether I'd slept. I said yes, and it was true. Then I asked him whether he knew where Ray had put the wrench Sam once lent him.

"What wrench?" he said.

I didn't know. The question had simply arrived, complete with a picture of the wrench. It was red, with the paint worn off near the jaw. I could even feel the cold metal in my hand.

I had felt Ray see that wrench as though I were looking straight at it. Then I remembered what Len had said. The wrench might not have been Ray's memory in the first place. He might have borrowed the picture from Len without even noticing.

I went to the sink and washed the cup. The cold water ran over my hands. I could see Sam, soaking wet, reaching up toward the dock. I could see a red wrench in somebody's hand, too.

I stood there for a while trying to work out which one I had actually seen.
