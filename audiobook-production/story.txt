NO FLOOR

A true story.

CHAPTER ONE. THE CABLE.

The steel cable is moving too fast for a man to hold.

It is the middle of the night, and a young Frenchman is standing on top of the South Tower of the World Trade Center with both hands full of trouble. The cable is supposed to go sideways, across to the North Tower. Instead, it has begun to go down.

Four hundred and forty pounds of braided steel are sliding out into the dark. Every second, more of it goes over the edge, and every foot that falls makes the rest harder to stop. On the opposite roof, two of the Frenchman's friends are holding the far end. They are being pulled toward the same edge, which has no fence.

The young man can hear the cable running. He can feel it pulling away. He knows what the sound will be if it all goes over.

He sees a clamp at the anchor. He has left it loose.

There are several things a sensible person might think about at a moment like this. He thinks about the wrench.

He dives for the clamp. He gets the wrench onto the bolt, and his hands do what he has practiced a thousand times, except that he has never practiced this. The clamp catches. The cable shudders. The movement stops.

For a few seconds nobody moves.

On the North Tower, his friends are still on their knees, holding steel. He is on the South Tower, listening to his own breathing. Between them, hundreds of feet of expensive equipment are hanging in the wrong place. In a few hours the sun will come up. They have not yet built the thing he came to New York to build.

He is twenty-four years old. His name is Philippe Petit. He is about to do something that will make him famous for the rest of his life, although at this particular moment a reasonable observer would rate his chances of finishing the night quite poorly.

Before we go on, you should know two things.

First, he is not being paid. There is no television deal. He has not arranged a sponsor, and the authorities have certainly not agreed to any of this. If he succeeds, the police will probably arrest him.

Second, he doesn't want to jump from one building to the other. He intends to take a walk.

The two roofs are roughly one hundred and forty feet apart. The street is one thousand three hundred and fifty feet below. The thing he means to walk on is a steel cable a little thinner than your thumb. He plans to stand on it with his own feet, carry a balancing pole, and go across, without a safety harness.

He intends to do this after a sleepless night moving heavy equipment through the dark on top of two unfinished skyscrapers.

The cable is in the wrong place. The clock is running. Far below, in the city that has no idea what's happening up here, people are driving taxis and making coffee and getting ready for the morning shift.

The cable really did get away from him. The forgotten clamp really did stop it. His friends really were almost dragged from the roof. Later, when Philippe told the story, he laughed about how close they had come to losing everything.

I can't imagine laughing. Perhaps you have to be on the roof.

But there is another part of the story I find even harder to understand. He had waited years for this night, risked the safety of people who trusted him, and watched nearly half a ton of steel come within seconds of disaster.

Then he went back to work.

CHAPTER TWO. THE DENTIST.

Six years earlier, Philippe Petit was sitting in a dentist's waiting room in France when he saw a newspaper photograph of two buildings that did not exist yet.

In New York, builders were going to put up twin towers. Very tall. Very close together. The illustration showed them standing side by side, with a strip of sky between them.

Philippe was a street performer. He juggled. He did magic tricks. He rode a unicycle. And he walked the high wire, which means that if somebody offered him a perfectly safe bridge, he would generally prefer something much narrower.

The picture in the newspaper gave him an idea. He didn't ask whether it was possible. He didn't ask permission. He tore the page out, left the dentist's office, and postponed having his tooth looked at.

I like to think about the rest of the people in that waiting room. Somebody may have had a terrible toothache. Somebody was worrying about the bill. Somebody else was looking for a way to fill ten minutes without talking to strangers. For one of them, the same cheap newspaper was about to take over the next six years of his life.

That's the first strange thing about this story. The towers had barely been built, and a man three thousand miles away was planning to do something with them that the architects had never imagined.

The architects designed buildings with doors, offices, elevators, electrical systems, and floors. Philippe saw two enormous posts for a wire.

He learned to cross cables high above ordinary ground, then tried more daring places. In Paris, he walked between the towers of Notre Dame. In Australia, he performed on the great bridge over Sydney Harbour. These were not auditions for the New York walk, but they became proof that he could put a foot where most people could not put their eyes.

He practiced constantly, although a high-wire artist's idea of practice is different from yours or mine. He had to know where his weight was without looking at his feet. He had to understand a cable's movement by feeling it through the soles of his shoes. He had to stay relaxed enough to make tiny corrections with his body while standing in a place where an ordinary person would be too frightened to move.

People tend to describe bravery as not being afraid. That makes the whole thing sound like a personality test. The more useful question is what a person has learned to do in the presence of fear.

In France, Philippe and his friends tried to rehearse the entire operation in a field. They stretched ropes and cable between trees. They practiced hauling lines, bringing heavy steel across a gap, and securing it without dropping anything. He needed the nighttime operation to become so familiar that he could do it while exhausted, cold, hurried, and unable to see the far end of the rope.

The rehearsals went badly. Hardware stuck. Lines snapped. Plans that looked perfectly sensible on paper collapsed when somebody actually tried them. He discovered that a problem solved in the afternoon could return, completely changed, in the dark.

He was also asking his friends to do something peculiar. They were not the ones who wanted to walk between the towers. They were being asked to smuggle equipment into buildings they didn't control, climb to the roofs, and spend a night handling a huge cable at a lethal height. Philippe's talent could not protect them from falling.

One of the people who helped him most was Jean-Louis Blondeau, a photographer from France who became his partner in the practical work. There were other friends, too, people whose names tend to get lost when a famous picture shows only one man in the sky.

A wire walker is alone on the wire. He is almost never alone in the effort that gets it there.

By the beginning of nineteen seventy-four, the two towers were standing. The plan that had lived for years in sketches and practice fields was about to acquire a street address.

Philippe flew to New York.

He was twenty-four. He had very little money, no permission, and a construction project to learn from the inside without anyone finding out why.

He also had a magnificent advantage: people in cities are used to seeing strangers who seem to have somewhere to be.

CHAPTER THREE. ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR FLOORS.

There are easier places to practice being invisible than the World Trade Center in nineteen seventy-four. The buildings were enormous, new, and still full of construction activity. Every day, hundreds of people moved through their corridors carrying tools, papers, and equipment. A man who looked busy might be one more worker. Or he might be a man from France measuring the distance between the rooftops.

Philippe studied the place. He examined how the towers were being finished. He watched people entering and leaving. He paid attention to where elevators stopped and where the stairs began. He became familiar with the way the roofs were laid out and the beams that might bear the enormous strain of a steel wire pulled tight.

He once talked his way into an interview with Guy Tozzoli, the executive responsible for the World Trade Center, by pretending to be a journalist. Tozzoli was eager for publicity. The French visitor was interested in a very specific kind of information, including how the towers moved in the wind.

Consider that for a moment. A man could be standing in an office, discussing the greatest new office complex in New York, while secretly thinking about tying a tightrope to it. The person answering his questions had no reason to imagine the conversation taking place inside a heist.

And that's what the operation became. It had the equipment list of a circus, the planning demands of an engineering project, and the legal status of a burglary. The one thing that didn't fit the usual model was the loot.

There wasn't any.

The cable weighed around four hundred and forty pounds. The balancing pole was twenty-eight feet long and weighed forty-five pounds, so it had to be taken apart to travel. There were ropes, tools, and a bow and arrow. And there were men who had to get all of it to the top of a skyscraper without explaining what it was for.

In the days before the walk, there were delays, failed attempts, and changes of plan. Some of the crew were caught in ridiculous situations. Philippe himself became so worn down that he could scarcely think straight. By the evening of August sixth, the operation had to happen. They were running out of money, time, and chances to move unnoticed.

The two crews split.

Jean-Louis and another accomplice went into the North Tower dressed as office workers. Philippe and two others entered the South Tower with heavy packages. The south crew traveled by elevator as far as they could, then moved the equipment higher by hand. At one point, they had to get hundreds of pounds of steel up a long set of stairs.

Imagine carrying a refrigerator upstairs. Now imagine that the refrigerator is made of cable, the stairs lead toward the roof of the tallest building in the city, and you cannot tell a security guard what you are doing.

They reached the unfinished upper levels and hid. They had to wait for darkness and for the roof to become clear.

By then the city below had turned into thousands of squares of light. Cars traveled in little red and white streams. Elevators carried people to offices where they would stay late, writing letters and looking at figures. The tower itself was still unfinished, with exposed metal where the exterior would later be closed in.

The roofs were separated by an enormous hole in the air. In the daylight, it could look like a neat gap between two buildings. At night, from the edge, it became something else. The streetlights did not illuminate the depth. There was simply a distance the human body could not cross, and a lot of black space underneath.

The plan called for a very light line to go across first. That line would pull over a heavier rope, which would pull another. Only then could the men begin transferring the steel cable.

They needed a way to send that first light line across one hundred and forty feet of open air.

They had brought a bow.

They had brought an arrow.

And on the far side of the gap, two friends were waiting in the dark.

CHAPTER FOUR. AN ARROW OVER NEW YORK.

Close your eyes for one second. Picture the two World Trade Center towers at midnight in nineteen seventy-four. No floodlights on the roof. No television helicopters. No cheering crowd. One team is on one building, one team on the other, and the only reliable connection between them is a radio.

Jean-Louis Blondeau takes the bow and shoots an arrow across the gap. A thread of fishing line follows it. The idea is so simple it sounds like a schoolyard stunt.

It works.

Mostly.

The arrow reaches the South Tower, but lands below the top edge on an unfinished metal truss. Philippe cannot simply reach over and pick it up. He has to climb down onto part of the building that slopes out over the drop, feel around in the darkness, and bring the line back up.

He still has a cable to bring across, and his feet are already on the wrong side of a roof.

He retrieves the line. The men start drawing larger ropes across the gap. This takes time. Every time one line is exchanged for a heavier one, the two crews have to handle it without letting go, tangling it, or making enough noise to attract attention. A cable that is merely inconvenient on the ground becomes frightening when the far end is a hundred and forty feet away and there are no handrails.

At last, the main steel cable begins to move across.

Then something goes wrong.

Philippe loses control of the cable. Its weight starts pulling it into the gap. On the North Tower, the crew tries to hold on and is nearly dragged over the edge. The wire races through Philippe's hands. The forgotten clamp is his last chance.

This is where we began.

He gets the wrench onto the bolt. The clamp bites down. The cable stops.

It takes hours to recover the steel from its hanging position. The crews haul and strain in the darkness. Nothing is going quickly now. The sky is beginning to lose its blackness, and time has started to become another enemy.

When they finally have the cable roughly in place, they still need to anchor it and draw it tight. Philippe is exhausted. His friends are exhausted. The whole night's work has been a series of things almost working, then nearly going disastrously wrong.

One detail is worth hanging on to: a walk cable is not a bridge. It does not sit still just because you have fastened both ends. It sags. It sways. The towers themselves move slightly with wind and temperature. In the middle, the cable has a life of its own. Philippe's feet will have to learn what kind of life it has before he reaches the other building.

That is not a problem the people on the roofs can solve for him. They can pull the cable tight, steady it with guy lines, and check the anchors. After that, he has to discover the answer by standing on it.

The light changes. New York wakes up.

In the streets, people start arriving for work. They buy coffee and carry briefcases. Buses stop. Delivery trucks reverse toward loading bays. Anyone who happens to look up sees two familiar shapes made stranger by morning light.

On the South Tower, a twenty-four-year-old Frenchman takes out a yellow grease pencil and marks a steel beam with his name, a symbol, and the date.

He has reached the part of the plan everyone will remember.

He picks up the long balancing pole.

Then he walks toward the edge.

CHAPTER FIVE. FIRST FOOT.

Picture the first foot.

Not because we have trouble imagining a man standing on a wire. We have seen tightrope acts before. We know there will be a long pole, bent knees, little movements of the ankles. Somewhere inside the picture, we expect an audience and a net.

Here, the audience is still eating breakfast. There is no net. The wire stretches from one steel building to another with a quarter of a mile of air below it.

At about seven fifteen in the morning, Philippe Petit places one foot on the cable and shifts his weight away from the roof.

He has spent years preparing for this. He knows where his hands belong on the balancing pole. He knows that the pole will slow the body's tendency to tip, giving him a little more time to correct. He knows to keep his eyes in the distance and use small changes of pressure under his feet.

The first step tells him something about the wire that no diagram could. It is not rigged as well as he would like. The cable has movement in it, and the buildings are part of that movement. But there is enough support beneath his foot to continue.

He takes another step.

And another.

The maddening thing about watching extraordinary skill is that it can look almost ordinary. The person on the wire does not appear to be trying very hard. His body has become extremely good at solving a problem that our bodies would struggle to define. It is making corrections before we would notice the need for them.

Philippe gets farther from the South Tower. The steel beam is no longer within reach. He is committed to the crossing.

A person down on the sidewalk sees a moving speck between the buildings. They stop. Another person looks up to see what the first person is looking at. Soon a little group forms. The people at the back cannot tell what everyone is watching, so they move around until they can see the sky.

Then they see him.

An extremely small man moving across an extremely large emptiness.

Somebody must think he is going to die. It is difficult to imagine another first thought. Then the figure keeps moving, not stumbling or waving for help, but walking with the calm deliberation of someone crossing a quiet street.

The city has a remarkable habit of continuing with its business. The cars keep moving. Offices open. A radio plays behind a shop counter. But more and more people stop and look up. There is no announcement. The man on the wire has bought no advertising. The crowd is assembling itself, person by person, because every new witness makes the sight worth looking for.

Philippe reaches the North Tower.

He has done it.

Here is the moment when any sensible ending would come. The young man climbs onto the roof. His friends grab him. They congratulate him, take the equipment apart, and go downstairs to meet the police. Later, newspapers print the astonishing photograph. Everyone agrees that once was enough.

Except the walk does not end there.

Philippe turns around.

He steps back onto the wire.

CHAPTER SIX. THE POLICE HAVE A PROBLEM.

Guy Tozzoli, the executive who had been so friendly to the supposed French journalist, learns about the walk through a police radio in his car. Someone reports that a man is walking on a tightrope between the towers.

The first thing he does is make sure the officers understand the obvious priority: don't let the man fall.

You would think that instruction went without saying. At that moment it was just about the only instruction anybody could give.

The officers sent to the roof find a situation for which there is no useful police training. They can see the man. They can shout at him. They cannot reach him. A man one thousand three hundred and fifty feet up has become, for the moment, beyond the practical authority of everyone waiting on the building.

Sergeant Charles Daniels, one of the responding officers, watches Philippe dancing on the wire. Philippe sees the police on the roof. Instead of rushing toward them, he smiles, bows, and gives them more of the performance. He drops to a knee. He lies down on the wire. At times, according to Daniels's account, his feet leave the cable and then settle back onto it.

The officer is supposed to bring him in. He is also looking at something he knows he may never see again. For a little while, he does the only sensible thing and watches.

Down below, the crowd is enormous now. At that height, people are too far away for Philippe to see their faces. He can hear them, though. When he bows or kneels, a sound rises from the street and reaches him later, after it has traveled all the way up through the air.

Think what that delay means. He moves; then he hears the city answer. From up there, even applause has to make a journey.

He crosses the gap again. And again. Eventually he lies on his back on the wire and looks up at the sky. The man who was meant to be fighting for survival seems to be enjoying the most peaceful morning in New York.

This is the part of the story that changes it for me.

Getting across once would have been an astounding stunt. A stunt is a thing you survive and then tell people about. Philippe had crossed successfully, proved the engineering, and earned his photograph. He was free to get off.

He went back because he liked being there.

He was not trying to escape an enemy, break a record for a cash prize, or win an argument. He had constructed a strange, delicate place in the sky and had discovered that he wanted to spend more time in it.

You do not have to admire his judgment to appreciate the absurd beauty of that choice. His friends had worked all night. His life depended on equipment they had almost lost in the dark. People were standing on the rooftops wondering how to stop him without killing him.

And he was lying on the wire, looking at the sky.

For the better part of an hour, a city famous for rushing found itself standing still to watch a man who had nowhere urgent to go.

CHAPTER SEVEN. EIGHT TIMES.

The number usually given is eight crossings. The time is about forty-five minutes. Both figures come from Philippe and the people who watched him, and they tell you almost nothing about what those minutes felt like.

Forty-five minutes is a long time to stand in the shower thinking about what you should have said in an argument. It is not very long for a concert or a football game. It is an eternity for a police officer waiting for someone to step off a rope suspended above a city.

Somewhere during those crossings, the walk changes from a problem to a performance. At first, the question is whether he can do it. After a while, people begin asking why he doesn't stop. The danger has not gone away. Yet it is no longer the only thing worth seeing.

The balancing pole dips. His knees bend. He turns. He bows to the people below. He is not watching the street. He is making his own little arrangement with gravity, one movement at a time.

There is an element of mischief in this. Philippe has never been a particularly obedient man. He has brought a great deal of work to a place he was forbidden to enter. Now, when the authorities finally catch sight of him, he has made himself impossible to catch. He can stay there until he decides to come back.

His friends, meanwhile, have no choice but to wait. Their arms ache. Their nerves are raw. They have been involved in a spectacular success that could become a terrible disaster at any instant.

This is why a famous photograph can tell a cruel little lie. It shows the artist alone, balanced against the world. The photograph has no room for the exhausted people holding ropes on the rooftops. It cannot show the preparation, the nearly lost cable, or the friend who had to spend hours learning how to move a machine through the dark so that one man could walk for forty-five minutes.

None of that makes Philippe's walk less extraordinary. It makes it harder to reduce to a single brave moment.

Eventually he returns to a roof for the last time and gives himself up to the people waiting. He is taken into custody. In the photographs afterward, he looks less like a criminal than a young man who has just got away with something wonderful.

The authorities could have made his punishment the most famous part of the story. Instead, in the face of widespread delight at what had happened, the formal charges were dropped. Philippe was asked to perform for children in Central Park. The Port Authority later gave him a lifetime pass to the South Tower's observation deck.

It is an odd result. He had entered the buildings without permission, put his helpers and himself in danger, and interrupted an ordinary workday in New York. The reward was permission to come back.

It is difficult to make a tidy moral out of that decision. The city had seen something stranger than an illegal stunt.

The officials responsible for those buildings had spent years getting them finished. They were monuments to planning, money, steel, and enormous ambition. Philippe showed up with a bag of tricks and a four-hundred-and-forty-pound cable and turned them, for forty-five minutes, into a piece of theater.

The buildings were still office buildings when it was over. They simply meant something different to the people who had watched him.

CHAPTER EIGHT. NO FLOOR.

People often ask what he was thinking about up there. It's a fair question, although it usually receives a disappointing answer.

People who do difficult physical things are not always occupied by extraordinary thoughts. A pianist at the keyboard may be thinking about a thumb. A climber on a mountain may be thinking about a loose bootlace. A driver at two hundred miles an hour may be thinking about the next corner and nothing else.

Philippe was thinking about a wire. His hands and feet were busy. His eyes had work to do. The more astonishing the performance seemed from the street, the more practical it was from where he stood.

There is something oddly reassuring about that. The people below saw one thousand three hundred and fifty feet of death. The person on the wire saw a place to put his next foot.

In two thousand and one, twenty-seven years after the walk, the World Trade Center towers were destroyed in the attacks of September eleventh. The buildings Philippe had made famous in an entirely different way were gone, along with thousands of lives. Nothing in this story should make that tragedy feel like part of a neat ending. It wasn't.

I think of the towers for many reasons. One of them is an old photograph of a tiny figure in the space between them. The photograph is strange now because the thing at its center is the only part of the scene that seems to belong entirely to the past. The city has changed. The towers have vanished. The man, in that one frame, is still taking his next step.

I used to think the remarkable thing was that Philippe got away with it. He broke into buildings, took a risk no responsible person would recommend, and came down with the city's applause in his ears. What a fantastic escape from ordinary life.

But that explanation misses something. Escapes are usually about getting away from a place you don't want to be. Philippe had spent years trying to get to a place nobody else wanted to stand.

Once he was there, he stayed.

There's a detail from that morning I keep returning to. For part of the performance, Philippe lay down on the wire and looked at the sky.

The crowd below must have thought he was showing off. The police thought he might die. His friends must have wanted him to get off before the cable moved again. The whole city was looking up at him.

And he was looking up, too.

For a few minutes, the man on the wire and the people watching him were all looking in the same direction.

Up.

THE END.
